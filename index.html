What I build

Four layers, bottom to top.

The agent work sits on top of the integration work. That order matters — it's why the agents have anything trustworthy to act on.

  • 01

    Agent control planes

    Mandate-driven development control planes, plus the governance, skills and operating tooling that decide what an agent is allowed to do, what it must prove, and what it can never touch. Written in TypeScript and Go.

  • 02

    Document AI & intelligent capture

    Extraction, validation and hand-off pipelines for invoices and financial documents — portal configuration, custom extensions, and the tooling around them, wired straight into downstream ERP and procurement systems.

  • 03

    A dozen-plus production integrations between procurement and ERP platforms: payments, tax determination, cost-centre and master-data sync, purchase-order flows and AP automation — across retail, fintech, logistics and services.

  • 04

    The unglamorous things that make the above usable: inventory cycle-count web and native mobile apps, credential and client vaults, schema-inference utilities, and internal developer tooling.

How I got here

Two decades of shipping, mostly unglamorous.

I wrote my first line of code at fifteen. By sixteen it was running a business — a video rental shop: inventory, memberships, late fees, and a queue of people at the counter who needed it to work right now. Nobody told me that was unusual, so I kept going.

I never finished the bachelor's. I did finish a technician's diploma at SENAI, and somewhere around dropping out I won the university coding competitions — both divisions, rookie and veteran, while still a rookie myself. Pascal and C, algorithms written to be tested rather than admired. It's still the credential I'd point at.

What came after was less romantic and more useful. Networks and machines kept alive in a college lab, then years of advanced network engineering at Siemens, where the racks ran Solaris and a link flapping at three in the morning was somebody's outage by nine. ERP posting routines, where a bug and a misstated ledger are the same event. Branch rollouts across a retail bank, where the deadline is Monday morning and there is no version of done that skips the branch in the middle of nowhere.

At the bank I automated my own job. Batch walked the agency networks, inventoried every device, and produced the spreadsheet procurement bought from. Evidence gathered automatically, a decision made on deterministic data, a person approving. Nobody called that an agent in 2010. It is the same loop I build today.

Then mobile payments for taxis at the very start of that era. Systems for a state department of education. Budget control for public highway projects. ERP for chemicals, for lighting, for logistics. Eventually CTO, with a large technical team. I've led people for years and never wanted the version of it where you stop building — I lead from the floor, not from the top.

Then almost seven years abroad. We moved to the Netherlands — a year at a startup first, then close to six as NetSuite architect for Optiver, one of the world's leading trading firms. Every entity of the company onto one platform, China included, procure-to-pay automated across regions, bank feeds wired up country by country. Finance teams in Amsterdam, Chicago and Shanghai, all needing the same ledger to behave the same way on the same Monday. Different time zones, different regulators, no version of "works on my machine" that survives any of it.

The detour that kept coming back

The founder who did not want to be one.

I tried owning a business once. It failed, and taught me more about cash, margin and the distance between a plan and month-end than any job had. For years I treated that as a useful correction: I was a builder, an architect, sometimes a leader — but not a company owner.

Whenever entrepreneurship returned, I found a sensible reason to refuse it. The timing was wrong. The risk was unnecessary. I already had good work. There was always a respectable argument for remaining where I was.

God was less interested in my categories. The work kept finding me, the responsibility kept growing, and doors opened that did not fit inside an employment contract. I resisted it for years, until the decision stopped being about ambition and became a matter of obedience.

Cabral Systems did not begin because I had always dreamed of becoming an entrepreneur. It began when I finally stopped arguing with the direction God had been giving me. I am still a builder first; now I am also responsible for what gets built, whom it serves, and the company formed around it.

I also spent a while teaching people to use computers, which is where I learned that if you can't explain a system plainly, you don't understand it.

None of that is the most important thing about me. I'm a husband, a father of three, and before any of it a servant of Jesus. I teach the Bible at Sunday school in my Baptist church when I'm not in a terminal. A year ago we left the Netherlands and moved back to Brazil — that was God's plan rather than mine, and I've stopped being surprised by the difference. It shows up in the work too: I would rather tell a client the true thing than the profitable thing, and I would rather a system be trustworthy than clever.

So the route into finance systems didn't come from finance, and it didn't come from a consultancy. It came from underneath — the networks, the hardware, the integrations, the rollouts that had to hold. That's why I'm willing to put agents near these systems now, and exactly why I'm careful about which parts.
